# Frameworks

The methods the agent reasons with. Add your own at the end.

## Evidence hierarchy

**Use when:** Whenever two sources disagree, or before you recommend cutting or scaling anything.

Not all evidence weighs the same. Rank it, and do not make a big call on the lowest rung alone.

1. Rank the evidence you have, from strongest to weakest: controlled tests, then store or CRM actuals, then customer survey answers, then platform-reported attribution, then anecdote.
2. When two rungs disagree, lean on the higher one and say why. Then name what would settle it.
3. Never cut or scale a channel on the lowest rung alone. You need one rung above it to agree.
4. Say which rung every number comes from. A reader should be able to see how much to trust it.

## Capture versus create

**Use when:** When a channel looks very efficient, especially brand search, retargeting, and email.

Some channels create demand. Others collect demand that already exists. Judge them differently.

1. Label each channel as mostly creating demand (reaching people who did not know you) or mostly capturing it (reaching people who already do).
2. Judge creating channels on new customers and on what customers say, over a longer window.
3. Judge capturing channels on cost and on whether they hold or lose volume. A very high return is a warning, not a win.
4. Protect capture spend at its current level. Do not scale it on return alone. Only reduce it through a proper holdout test.

## Test design checklist

**Use when:** Before you recommend any test, and before you log one.

A test that cannot say 'go' or 'stop' in advance is just a change.

1. Write one hypothesis in a single sentence. It should name the change and the result you expect.
2. Change one thing only.
3. Pick one main metric and say where it comes from.
4. Set the success rule and the stop rule before the test starts. Include a number and a time.
5. Check the test log first. Do not repeat a test that has already answered the question.
6. Allow for lag. If the real outcome takes weeks, use a leading metric and say so.
7. Decide now what you will do for each result: win, lose, or inconclusive.
8. Log the test, then log what was learned when it ends.

## Claim, proof, and customer language

**Use when:** When you check whether what the brand says matches what customers say and value.

Every claim in market should be in the customer's language, backed by proof, and different from the alternatives.

1. List every claim the brand makes in market: ads, site, emails, and sales conversations.
2. For each claim, check whether customers use similar words when they explain why they bought.
3. Check for proof. Is there evidence in reviews, results, or case material that backs the claim?
4. Check for difference. Would a competitor be able to say the same thing?
5. Score each claim from 0 to 2 on customer language, proof, and difference. Flag any claim that scores 0 on customer language.
6. List what customers value that the brand does not say yet. That is usually where the next message comes from.

## Reading a long sales cycle

**Use when:** For services, B2B, or any business where deals take weeks or months to close.

Recent weeks have no closed outcomes yet. Do not read missing wins as failure.

1. Group leads by the week they arrived, not by the week they closed.
2. Judge recent weeks on leading measures: qualified leads, meetings, and proposals.
3. Judge win rate only on groups old enough to have closed. Say how many proposals sit behind it.
4. Treat any group with fewer than 10 outcomes as directional only.
5. Look at what changed in the lead process before you judge a source. A form change can move volume and quality at once.
